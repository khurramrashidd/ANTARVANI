A camera is the only sensor.

अन्तर्वाणी — the voice within

Twenty Hindi care phrases, read from the movement of the mouth alone.

People who cannot speak — after a laryngectomy, with a tracheostomy, during intensive care — are often left pointing at a card. ANTARVANI reads the phrase off their lips with an ordinary Android phone. No microphone, no electrodes, nothing worn on the face.

How it works Read the abstract

Losing speech does not mean losing language.

The words are still there. What is missing is a way out of the body that other people can hear — and the tools meant to help are mostly built for English, for a keyboard, or for a sensor nobody wants taped to their throat.

Boards are slow

Pointing at a laminated card gets a thirsty patient a glass of water in the time it takes to find the picture. Most boards carry a dozen symbols and nothing a person would actually say.

Sensors are intrusive

Surface electrodes, throat microphones and ultrasound probes all work, and all require equipment on the face or neck of someone already having a hard day.

Hindi is underserved

Published lip-reading research is overwhelmingly English. A hospital ward in Maharashtra needs the phrases people there say, in the language they say them in.

Twenty phrases that matter at a bedside.

Chosen for need, not for how easy they are to tell apart. Each is a whole utterance, said in a three-second window, so the system learns a phrase rather than stitching one together from letters.

Camera to phrase, entirely on the phone.

Nothing is uploaded at the moment of use, and no audio is captured at any stage of the project — not while gathering recordings, not while the app is being used.

Record

Three seconds from the front camera. The microphone is never opened.

Find the face

The phone locates the face and tracks the landmarks around the mouth, frame by frame.

Align and crop

The picture is straightened and scaled so the mouth fills the same area whether the person sat close to the camera or far from it.

Recognise

A small neural network reads the sequence of mouth shapes and decides which phrase it was.

Personalise

A few recordings from the person themselves teach it their own way of making those shapes.

Speak

The phrase is read aloud in Hindi by the phone.

We recorded our own data, from scratch.

No collection of Hindi lip-reading video existed for this, so we built one. Everything the system knows, it learned from recordings made by this team, with consent, on ordinary phones.

A purpose-built recording app

We wrote an Android application specifically for gathering the recordings. It handles the consent paperwork, gives each volunteer a number instead of a name, walks them through the phrases one at a time, checks each recording on the phone before accepting it, and uploads in the background. Every clip arrives named, attributed and checkable.

Checked before it counts

A recording is only kept if the face stayed in frame, the lighting was good enough and the phrase finished inside the three seconds. Sessions that failed the check are set aside rather than quietly mixed in, and the people who collected the recordings reviewed them a second time by hand.

The recordings and the measured results are not on this page.
They are private project data. The team can see the live collection progress, the recordings themselves and the measured accuracy on the team dashboard, after signing in.

ANTARVANI: A Camera-Only Silent Speech Interface for Hindi Care Phrases with Few-Shot Personalisation

B.Tech Computer Science & Engineering major project, Amity School of Engineering and Technology, Amity University Mumbai, 2026–27.

Be one of the voices.

Every phrase the system understands was recorded by a person who volunteered three minutes of their time. If you are in or near Navi Mumbai and would like to take part, leave your details and someone from the team will contact you to arrange a session.

Register as a volunteer

Languages you speak

What taking part involves

  1. We meet you somewhere convenient and explain the project.
  2. You sign a short consent form. It has a number on it; your name stays on that form and goes nowhere else.
  3. You say twenty short Hindi phrases to a phone camera, silently — mouthing them, no sound. Three seconds each.
  4. That is it. About fifteen minutes in total.

What we record, and what we don't

Video of your face only, from the front camera, in three-second clips. The microphone is never switched on — not during the session, not at any point in the project.

In everything we write down you are a number, not a name. Nobody under eighteen is recorded. You can ask us to delete your recordings afterwards and we will, and the deletion is logged.

Rules we set before the first recording.

No audio, ever

The microphone is not opened while gathering recordings or while the app is in use. A silent speech interface that recorded sound would be collecting exactly the thing its users cannot produce, and exactly the thing a hospital ward has no right to capture.

Consent, per person

Every volunteer signs a numbered consent form before recording, and separately decides whether their recordings may ever be published. Nobody under eighteen is recorded. A volunteer can ask for their recordings to be deleted from inside the app, and the deletion is logged.

Names are not data

Volunteers are a number everywhere the project writes anything down. Names live in a private list the team signs in to see. They are never part of the dataset and never on this page.

The phone, not the cloud

Recognition runs on the device. Recordings are uploaded only while the corpus is being gathered, to a folder the project controls, and never at the moment somebody is using the app to say they are in pain.
